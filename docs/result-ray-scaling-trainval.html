Ray scaling benchmark: v1.0-trainval

Nov 23, 2026 · build log §8 · v1.0-mini benchmark

How ingestion's wall time changes with the number of Ray workers on the full trainval split (850 scenes), next to v1.0-mini (10 scenes) on the same 32-CPU machine. Trainval's best is 4.55× faster than one worker, at 32 workers; mini's is 1.26×, at 8. The export from the sweep's last run passed every completeness check (2,759,967 rows).

How it was measured

  • One machine: every run of both datasets on the same Runpod CPU pod with 32 CPUs, so the dataset is the only thing that differs.
  • Runs: each worker count (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32) run 3 times as a fresh process, startup included, with one scene per batch and at most max(32, 2 × workers) tasks in flight. The harness is the same as for the v1.0-mini page.
  • CPU use: read from the pod's own share of the host, so 100% means all 32 of its CPUs busy.
  • Not compared with the laptop: the v1.0-mini page's results come from a different machine, so its times are not set against these.

Wall time and time after startup

Every run is a fresh partition.py process, timed by the benchmark from launch to exit and split where the first task starts on a worker:
  • Wall time \(T\): from launching the process to its exit. This is what you actually wait for.
  • Startup \(S\): from launch to the first task starting: Python and imports, ray.init() starting the workers, loading the devkit's tables, and building the first batch's input. It happens once per run, before anything is parallel, so more workers can't shrink it.
  • Time after startup \(T - S\): the rest of the run. Mostly the tasks running across the workers, but also the driver building each later batch's input one at a time, collecting every task's result, and shutting down.
Each worker count runs several times, and the tables give means over its successful runs. For a worker count with \(n\) runs, where \(T_i\) and \(S_i\) are run \(i\)'s wall time and startup: \[\bar{T} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} T_i \qquad \bar{S} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} S_i \qquad \text{time after startup} = \bar{T} - \bar{S}\] Stdev is the sample standard deviation of the wall times, \(s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n} (T_i - \bar{T})^2}\): how much the repeats differ from each other.

Speedup vs. worker count

Two speedups per dataset, each relative to its own 1-worker runs, where \(T_N\) and \(S_N\) are the mean wall time and mean startup with \(N\) workers (see Wall time and time after startup): \[\text{wall clock} = \frac{T_1}{T_N} \qquad \text{after startup} = \frac{T_1 - S_1}{T_N - S_N}\] The dashed line is ideal linear scaling.

Observations

  • v1.0-mini was fastest at 8 workers, 1.26× the 1-worker time. Past that it slowed down: from 8 to 32 workers its wall time rose 7.8 s, 6.9 s of it from a longer startup (starting more workers). With 10 scenes there are only 10 batches of work to share out, so beyond a few workers there's little left for them to do. Leaving out startup, its time after startup was 1.86× faster at 8 workers.
  • v1.0-trainval was fastest at 32 workers, 4.55× the 1-worker time, the largest count tested, and still improving. Leaving out startup, its time after startup was 8.06× faster at 32 workers.
  • The gap between each dataset's solid and dotted lines is its fixed startup cost: more workers shrink the time after startup, but not the startup.

Where the time goes

Each bar is one worker count's mean wall time, split into startup and the time after it; the two happen one after the other, so they add up to the whole run. The time after startup is mostly the tasks running across the workers, but it also holds the driver building each later batch's input one at a time, collecting results and shutting down. The datasets get separate charts because their times differ about 15 times at 1 worker.

Observations

  • v1.0-mini: startup is 67% of the run at 1 worker and 80% at 32 workers.
  • v1.0-trainval: startup is 10% of the run at 1 worker and 49% at 32 workers.
  • Startup grows with the dataset, because it includes loading the devkit's tables: 24 s for v1.0-mini against 52 s for v1.0-trainval at 1 worker. No number of workers removes it, so it is what caps the wall-clock speedup above.

Per-task time breakdown

Each task's time, averaged over every task in every repeat:
  • Wait: from the driver submitting the task to it starting on a worker, i.e. queueing for a free worker.
  • Compute: building and projecting the batch's rows.
  • Disk write: time inside pq.write_table.
Each batch has two tasks, a light one writing the core tables and a heavy one projecting boxes, and the averages cover both. Tasks run at the same time on different workers, so these don't add up to a run's wall time; the chart above does that. Each panel has its own scale.

Observations

  • v1.0-mini: wait falls from 5.55 s to 2.46 s between 1 and 32 workers, while compute rises from 0.379 s to 2.200 s (5.8×).
  • v1.0-trainval: wait falls from 8.72 s to 0.86 s between 1 and 32 workers, while compute rises from 0.248 s to 0.607 s (2.5×).
  • Part of the rise is a one-off cost. A worker's first task is slower, most likely because it also loads the pipeline's code on that worker: on v1.0-trainval at 1 worker it took 1.40 s against 0.247 s for later tasks, and 4.84 s at 32 workers, where 32 workers load at once. More workers means more first tasks. v1.0-mini has only 20 tasks per run, so at 32 workers nearly every task is a worker's first, which accounts for most of its rise.
  • The rest is tasks slowing each other down. Leaving out first tasks, v1.0-trainval's compute still rises from 0.247 s to 0.526 s (2.1×) between 1 and 32 workers, though each task does the same work. The pod averaged 49% busy over the time after startup at 32 workers, so tasks aren't short of CPUs; they are probably competing for something shared, such as memory bandwidth, which these runs don't measure. This is part of why the speedup after startup stays below ideal.
  • Disk isn't a bottleneck. Writes average at most 37 ms per task, for either dataset at any worker count.
